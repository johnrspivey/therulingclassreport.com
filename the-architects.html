The Architects

The officials who built, authorized, and sustained the political intelligence operation against a sitting president — documented from congressional testimony, inspector general reports, and court records.

← Return to Museum

What follows is not speculation about motives. It is a documented account of what specific officials did — in their official capacity, with the resources of the United States government — and what the formal record shows about those actions. Every finding cited below comes from a federal inspector general, a congressional investigation, a court filing, or a declassified intelligence document.

The Intelligence Layer
CIA Director
John Brennan
2013 – 2017 · Obama Administration

Brennan was the central figure in the intelligence community's assessment of Russian election interference and one of the most aggressive public voices promoting the collusion narrative after leaving office. He stated publicly on multiple occasions that he had seen evidence of Trump-Russia collusion.

Documented Finding

Brennan's own handwritten notes, declassified by DNI Ratcliffe in 2020, show he briefed President Obama in July 2016 about intelligence suggesting Clinton had approved a plan to manufacture the Russia collusion narrative as a distraction from her email server scandal. Brennan then referred the matter to the FBI — which opened Crossfire Hurricane. Mueller found no collusion. The Durham Report found the investigation lacked a verified predicate.

Brennan's security clearance was revoked by President Trump in 2018. He has never been charged. He continues to appear regularly as an intelligence analyst on cable news.

Sources: Brennan handwritten notes — DNI declassification, Oct. 2020 · Durham Special Counsel Report (2023) · Mueller Report (2019)
Director of National Intelligence
James Clapper
2010 – 2017 · Obama Administration

Clapper co-signed the January 2017 Intelligence Community Assessment attributing election interference to Russia and expressing "high confidence" in its conclusions. He became a prominent media voice promoting the collusion narrative through 2019.

Documented Finding

Clapper testified to Congress in March 2017 that he had seen no evidence of collusion between the Trump campaign and Russia — directly contradicting his cable news statements. He was also referred to DOJ by congressional investigators for allegedly misleading Congress in 2013 about NSA bulk collection of Americans' phone records. He was never charged for either matter.

Sources: Senate Judiciary Committee referral (2013) · Clapper Congressional testimony, March 2017 · Senate Intelligence Committee report (2020)
The FBI Layer
FBI Director
James Comey
2013 – 2017 · Status updated Oct. 27, 2026

Comey oversaw both the Clinton email server investigation, in which he recommended no charges, and the Crossfire Hurricane investigation into the Trump campaign. President Trump fired him in May 2017.

Documented Findings

The DOJ Inspector General found that Comey violated FBI policy in how he handled memos documenting his conversations with President Trump, including sharing one with a Columbia University professor so its contents would reach the press and prompt a special counsel. The IG said he set a "dangerous example" for FBI employees. In a separate report, the IG found that Comey's public statements about the Clinton investigation, including his July 2016 press conference and his October 2016 letter to Congress, departed from Justice Department norms. The IG found no evidence that political bias drove the decision not to charge Clinton. DOJ declined to prosecute over the memos.

Record Update · Oct. 27, 2026

This card previously said "Comey was never charged." As of Oct. 27, 2026, he has been charged in three federal cases, detailed below. The earlier wording is kept here for the record.

What happened

  • DocumentedOn Oct. 26, 2026, Judge Trevor McFadden unsealed charges in D.C. federal court that were filed under seal on Oct. 5: transmitting national defense information and conspiracy to do so, under 18 U.S.C. § 793(d) and (g).
  • AllegedProsecutors say that in October 2016, as FBI Director, Comey directed staff to speak with New York Times reporter Charlie Savage about a Reuters report on Yahoo assisting U.S. email surveillance, and in doing so passed on classified information. Savage's story ran Oct. 5, 2016.
  • DocumentedProsecutors filed a criminal information, not a grand jury indictment. DOJ called it a "waiverless criminal information" filed to stop the clock on the 10-year statute of limitations. Normally a felony can't go forward on an information without the defendant's consent, which is why the filing itself is contested.
  • DisputedComey's lawyers say the case is time-barred because there was no indictment within 10 years, and that two teams of prosecutors in the first Trump administration investigated the same article and brought no charges. They called it "the government's third attempt to prosecute Mr. Comey within the last 13 months." A judge will decide.
  • DocumentedThe earlier cases: a September 2025 indictment for false statements and obstruction was dismissed without a ruling on the facts. A case filed in North Carolina in April 2026, charging two felony counts of threatening the president over his "86 47" seashell post, remains pending and has been delayed until at least early 2027.

In his own words

Documented · House Intelligence Committee, March 20, 2017

Asked by Rep. Trey Gowdy whether it is against the law to disseminate classified information, Comey answered that it is, and called it a serious crime that should be investigated.

Context: he was answering general legal questions during a hearing on the Russia investigation and the leaks surrounding it. He was not describing his own conduct. The new charges fall under 18 U.S.C. § 793, the law on transmitting national defense information.

What's at stake

Two principles collide here. One is that the law on classified information should apply to the most powerful officials, not just low-level leakers. The other is that criminal charges shouldn't become a tool used against a president's critics. How this case is resolved, including whether the time limit holds, will shape how both principles are understood going forward.

The case each side makes

Those who support the prosecution say: Comey himself testified that leaking classified information is a serious crime. If the evidence shows he directed exactly that, the law should apply to a former FBI Director the same as anyone else. Earlier decisions not to charge don't mean no crime occurred, and the IG already found he mishandled sensitive material once.

Those who oppose the prosecution say: This is the third case against a longtime critic of the president in 13 months. Two teams of prosecutors already reviewed this exact matter and declined to charge. Bypassing a grand jury at the edge of the time limit is unusual, and the 2017 testimony was a general statement of the law, not an admission about his own actions.

Who's involved

  • James Comey: former FBI Director; says he is "still innocent."
  • Patrick Fitzgerald: Comey's attorney, a former U.S. Attorney.
  • Charlie Savage: the New York Times reporter on the Oct. 5, 2016 Yahoo story.
  • Judge Trevor McFadden: the federal judge who unsealed the case.
  • Trey Gowdy: the congressman who questioned Comey about leaks in 2017.

Questions to consider

  • Should Comey's 2017 statement of the law be held to his own alleged conduct, even though he wasn't speaking about himself?
  • If prosecutors reviewed this twice and declined, what would need to be new to justify charging it now?
  • Should time limits on prosecution bend for serious national security cases, or hold firm for everyone?
  • Does the number of cases against one person tell you something, and if so, what?

FBI Deputy Director
Andrew McCabe
2016 – 2018

McCabe was the number two official at the FBI during the critical period of both the Clinton investigation and the opening of Crossfire Hurricane. He was fired two days before he would have been eligible for his full pension.

Documented Finding

The DOJ Inspector General found that McCabe lied to federal investigators on four separate occasions — including lying to Comey himself — about leaking information to the Wall Street Journal. He was referred for criminal prosecution. DOJ declined to charge him in 2020. He now works as a CNN analyst.

Sources: DOJ IG Report — McCabe (2018) · DOJ declination letter (2020) · OPR referral documentation
FBI Counterintelligence / Special Counsel Team
Peter Strzok
Lead Agent, Crossfire Hurricane / Mueller Investigation

Strzok was the lead FBI agent on Crossfire Hurricane and was subsequently assigned to the Mueller special counsel team. He was removed from Mueller's team after the DOJ Inspector General discovered his text messages with FBI lawyer Lisa Page.

Documented Finding

Text messages between Strzok and Page, reviewed by the DOJ IG, documented explicit anti-Trump sentiment and a reference to an "insurance policy" against a Trump presidency — sent while Strzok was lead agent on the investigation targeting Trump's campaign. In one message, when Page asked if Trump could actually win, Strzok replied: "No. No he won't. We'll stop it." Strzok was fired from the FBI in 2018.

Sources: Strzok-Page text messages — Congressional release (2017) · DOJ IG Report (2018) · Strzok FBI termination letter (2018)
FBI Attorney
Kevin Clinesmith
Crossfire Hurricane Investigation

Clinesmith was the FBI attorney responsible for processing FISA warrant renewals for surveillance of Carter Page, a Trump campaign associate. His conduct was the clearest documented act of evidence falsification in the entire Crossfire Hurricane saga.

Documented Finding — Criminal Conviction

Clinesmith altered an email from the CIA — changing language indicating that Carter Page was a CIA source to language suggesting he was not — and submitted the altered document to the FISA Court to obtain a surveillance warrant. He pleaded guilty to making a false statement. He received probation and community service. His law license was temporarily suspended and later reinstated.

Sources: U.S. v. Clinesmith, guilty plea, D.C. District Court (2020) · Durham Special Counsel Report (2023) · DOJ IG FISA Report (2019)
The Political Layer
Secretary of State / Presidential Candidate
Hillary Clinton
2009–2013 / 2016 Campaign

Clinton operated a private email server during her tenure as Secretary of State, transmitting classified information through an unsecured system. When the FBI investigation was opened, her team destroyed evidence under congressional subpoena.

Documented Findings

The FBI found 110 emails containing classified information on Clinton's server. After a congressional subpoena was issued, her team used BleachBit to destroy approximately 33,000 emails and physically destroyed mobile devices. Separately, Clinton's campaign and the DNC paid Fusion GPS through law firm Perkins Coie to commission the Steele Dossier — the fabricated intelligence document that formed the basis of the FISA surveillance of the Trump campaign. The FEC fined the Clinton campaign and DNC for misreporting those payments. No criminal charges were filed for the server or the dossier funding.

Sources: FBI Clinton email investigation findings — Comey statement (2016) · DOJ IG Report — Clinton Investigation (2018) · FEC consent agreement — Clinton campaign / DNC (2022) · Durham Special Counsel Report (2023)
